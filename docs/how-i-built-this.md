# How I built this

The narrative behind JSCC: why it's shaped the way it is, what the eval rounds actually showed, and what building it with an AI agent looked like day to day. The evidence lives elsewhere — [evals/README.md](../evals/README.md) for every round and figure, [docs/lessons-learned.md](lessons-learned.md) for the distilled, defend-in-conversation lessons, [docs/gate-reviews.md](gate-reviews.md) for the review process. This doc is the story that connects them.

## Three ideas, demonstrated at once

**1. Eval-driven agent design.** Every LLM stage ships behind an eval suite whose bar lives in code, not in prose: 80% for extraction and scoring, 85% for routing, 75% for composition. Routing and composition also carry zero-tolerance gates — a situation that needed a person must never be drafted, and neither may a reply that needs a detail nobody recorded.

No `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` is configured for this project, so every prompt was checked by hand: completions captured from real Claude.ai chats and replayed through the harness's `--record`/`--replay` fixtures. That constraint shaped the whole practice — cheap proxy runs (a subagent standing in for a real chat) decided whether a real capture round was worth spending, but their output never entered a recorded fixture. That distinction mattered in practice, not just on paper: the routing suite's proxy runs scored 26/26 on a wording that then scored 23/26 on the very next real round, on exactly the kind of case the proxies had passed. The lesson generalized — proxies are a filter for whether to spend a round, never a substitute for one — and it's why every suite's published number traces back to a real chat, one fresh conversation per case.

The eval-round history is long and case-by-case; the full account, including every miss and why, is [evals/README.md](../evals/README.md). The short version: extraction landed at 33/38 (87%) after two failed rounds (56%, 75%) on the same fixtures — tuned, not held out, except for two T5 coverage-expansion cases captured after the prompt was frozen. Fit scoring went from 84% to a rubric-exposing 64% on a wider suite, then to 27/28 twice after the prompt and nine score ranges were corrected. Composition needed a prompt rewrite mid-project after a grader upgrade caught invented weekdays and relative dates that every earlier check had missed, then landed at 25/28 after two grader recalibrations disentangled real defects from an overly strict style-reuse check. Routing took five rounds to reach 26/26 on its original 26 cases, then dropped to 36/38 the moment 10 held-out and 2 hostile cases were added — the model missed exactly the case a code-level guard was built to catch. None of these numbers are held-out rates in the strict sense; the suite sizes were chosen from the standard-error arithmetic, not from a target pass rate, and every published figure says which round produced it. The [D9](design-principles.md#d9--llm-stages-are-split-extract--score-scorer-sees-raw-jd-too) split — extraction and scoring as separate calls — exists so a scoring regression can be diagnosed without wondering whether extraction drifted underneath it.

**2. Structural safety for dual-use data.** The tool runs against real personal data and against a synthetic fixture, and the safety guarantee is enforced by construction, not by remembering to be careful: two isolated databases stamped with a mode marker, and two egress points — a pre-commit scanner guarding git, an authenticated, redacting sanitizer guarding every LLM call — that share one definition of "personal" ([D7](design-principles.md#d7--dual-use-data-safety-structural-not-disciplinary), [D8](design-principles.md#d8--hard-line-on-personal-identity-in-llm-traffic)). Redaction runs *before* the payload is authenticated, so no caller can opt out of it, including the ones that forget to. The guarantee is stated narrowly on purpose: structured identifiers and known names, not free-text NER — an honest boundary rather than an overclaimed one.

**3. Knowing when not to automate.** The follow-up drafter routes anything non-routine to a briefing card instead of a prose draft ([D10](design-principles.md#d10--drafter-routing-first-routine-only-composition)): a routing classifier with a zero-tolerance gate on the one failure that matters (auto-drafting something that needed a human judgment call), a rule that sends any reply needing an unrecorded fact to a person, and a composer that can decline for the same reason rather than invent one. The drafter never sends anything on its own; its output is always a draft or a card for a person to act on. Deciding where automation should stop was the actual design work here, not a fallback for where the model fell short.

## The process of building this with an AI agent

JSCC shipped across six phases and 100+ slices, each running its own plan/execute/validate cycle, each ending in a commit (or a tight commit series) small enough to check and hand off on its own. The slice boundary turned out to matter more than the slice estimates: individual time estimates were often wrong, but the boundary itself gave the work a natural point for validation, a check-in, and a context-compaction handoff — a clean place to save state, reset, and pick the work back up.

Two review lenses ran at every phase boundary, not just at the end: an adversarial pass asking where the code could break, be bypassed, or leak data, and an outside-reviewer walkthrough asking how the repo would read to an engineering director skimming it cold. Both produced real findings — a DNS-rebinding path an earlier review had rated low-risk and closed, contact-name redaction the docs described and no caller actually performed, a schema bump that silently failed to migrate older databases. The worked findings are in [docs/gate-reviews.md](gate-reviews.md).

One correction happened in the open and is worth naming here rather than smoothing over: my live, stated-out-loud impression while building this was that gate and hardening work was "the other 80%" of the effort on top of the features. I didn't check that against anything until late in the project. [`scripts/active_time.py`](../scripts/active_time.py) sums the gaps between consecutive commits and splits the result by a keyword match on commit subjects; run against the full history, it puts the split at roughly 48% feature/eval/prompt work and 52% gate/hardening/docs work — close to 1:1, not 4:1. The corrected number is still a real finding (review work is roughly as large as the build itself, which most estimates don't budget for), it's just not the number that felt true in the moment. The full account, including what the script's own bias is in each direction, is lesson 8 in [docs/lessons-learned.md](lessons-learned.md).

That's the shape of the pattern across this project: state an impression, then look for the artifact that would prove or correct it, and publish whichever one is true. The distilled lessons from doing that repeatedly — what worked, what didn't, and what each would mean for a team — are in [docs/lessons-learned.md](lessons-learned.md).
